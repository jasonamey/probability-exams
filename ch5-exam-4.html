A driver parks at a meter and pays for exactly 60 minutes. How long the driver actually stays, \(X\) minutes, is equally likely to be anywhere between 0 and 90 minutes. A parking officer writes a $30 ticket only if the car is still there more than 10 minutes after the paid time runs out.

  1. Find the probability that the driver overstays the paid time at all.
  2. Find the expected number of paid minutes that go unused.
  3. Given that the driver overstays, find the probability that the overstay is more than 15 minutes.
  4. Find the expected ticket cost.
Solution

\(X\sim\text{Unif}(0,90)\), so \(f(x)=\tfrac1{90}\) on \((0,90)\).

(a) \(P\{X>60\}=\tfrac{30}{90}=\tfrac13\).

(b) Unused minutes are \(g(X)=\max(60-X,0)\). \(E[g(X)]=\int_0^{60}(60-x)\tfrac1{90}\,dx=\tfrac{1800}{90}=20\) minutes.

(c) \(P\{X>75\mid X>60\}=\dfrac{15/90}{30/90}=\tfrac12\).

(d) A ticket is written when \(X>70\): \(E[\text{cost}]=30\cdot P\{X>70\}=30\cdot\tfrac{20}{90}=\tfrac{20}{3}\approx\) $6.67.

8 points

Finishing times in a running club's marathon are normally distributed with mean 240 minutes and standard deviation 25 minutes.

  1. Find the probability that a randomly chosen runner finishes in under 3.5 hours.
  2. The club gives a badge to its slowest 10% of finishers for “endurance”. Above what time does a runner earn the badge?
  3. Given that a runner finished in under 3.5 hours, find the probability that they finished in under 200 minutes.
  4. Four runners are chosen independently. Find the probability that at least one of them finishes in under 3.5 hours.
Solution

(a) 3.5 hours is 210 minutes. \(z=\tfrac{210-240}{25}=-1.2\), so \(P=1-\Phi(1.2)=1-0.8849=0.1151\).

(b) Need \(P\{X>t\}=0.10\): \(t=240+1.28(25)=272\) minutes (4 h 32 min).

(c) \(P\{X<200\}=1-\Phi(1.6)=0.0548\). Since \(\{X<200\}\subset\{X<210\}\), the answer is \(\tfrac{0.0548}{0.1151}\approx0.4761\).

(d) \(1-(0.8849)^4\approx0.3868\).

8 points

A rectangle has perimeter 8. One side, \(X\), is chosen uniformly at random between 1 and 3, so the other side is \(4-X\). Let \(A=X(4-X)\) be the area.

  1. Find \(E[A]\).
  2. Find \(P\{A>3.75\}\).
  3. Find the CDF of \(A\). (First work out which values \(A\) can take.)
  4. Find the density of \(A\).
Solution

(a) \(E[X^2]=\mathrm{Var}(X)+E[X]^2=\tfrac{4}{12}+4=\tfrac{13}{3}\), so \(E[A]=4E[X]-E[X^2]=8-\tfrac{13}3=\tfrac{11}{3}\).

(b) Write \(A=4-(X-2)^2\). Then \(A>3.75\iff |X-2|<0.5\iff 1.5<X<2.5\), probability \(\tfrac{1}{2}\).

(c) \(A\) ranges over \([3,4]\). For \(3\le a\le4\): \(P\{A\le a\}=P\{|X-2|\ge\sqrt{4-a}\}\). Since \(X-2\sim\text{Unif}(-1,1)\), \(P\{|X-2|<s\}=s\) for \(0\le s\le1\). So \(F_A(a)=1-\sqrt{4-a}\) on \([3,4]\), 0 below 3 and 1 above 4. (The map is not one-to-one, so the CDF method is easier than the transformation theorem.)

(d) \(f_A(a)=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{4-a}}\) for \(3<a<4\), and 0 otherwise.

9 points

A detector near a weak radioactive source clicks at random moments, at an average rate of 4 clicks per minute, with counts in non-overlapping time intervals independent.

  1. Find the probability that the gap between two consecutive clicks is longer than 30 seconds.
  2. The detector has been silent for a full minute. Find the probability that it stays silent for at least another 30 seconds.
  3. Find the probability of exactly 3 clicks in a one-minute interval.
  4. After switching on, the detector's display records until the first click or until 45 seconds have passed, whichever comes first. Find the expected recording time in minutes.
Solution

The number of clicks in \(t\) minutes is Poisson with mean \(4t\), so the waiting time \(T\) to the next click is \(\text{Exp}(4)\) (minutes).

(a) \(P\{T>0.5\}=e^{-2}\approx0.1353\).

(b) By the memoryless property, \(P\{T>1.5\mid T>1\}=P\{T>0.5\}=e^{-2}\approx0.1353\).

(c) \(e^{-4}\tfrac{4^3}{3!}\approx0.1954\).

(d) \(E[\min(T,0.75)]=\int_0^{0.75}P\{T>t\}\,dt=\int_0^{0.75}e^{-4t}dt=\tfrac{1-e^{-3}}{4}\approx0.2376\) minutes. (Or by LOTUS: \(\int_0^{0.75}t\cdot4e^{-4t}dt+0.75\,e^{-3}\), which gives the same value.)

8 points

A lawn sprinkler throws drops of water. The distance \(X\) (in metres) from the sprinkler head to where a drop lands has density \(f(x)=c\,x(3-x)\) for \(0<x<3\), and 0 otherwise.

  1. Find \(c\).
  2. Find \(E[X]\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\).
  3. Find the probability that a drop lands within 1 metre of the head.
  4. Five drops land independently. Find the probability that at least two of them land within 1 metre of the head.
Solution

(a) \(\int_0^3 x(3-x)\,dx=\tfrac{27}{2}-9=\tfrac92\), so \(c=\tfrac29\).

(b) \(E[X]=\tfrac29\int_0^3x^2(3-x)dx=\tfrac29\cdot\tfrac{27}{4}=\tfrac32\). \(E[X^2]=\tfrac29\int_0^3x^3(3-x)dx=\tfrac29\cdot\tfrac{243}{20}=\tfrac{27}{10}\). \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=\tfrac{27}{10}-\tfrac94=\tfrac{9}{20}=0.45\).

(c) \(\tfrac29\int_0^1(3x-x^2)dx=\tfrac29\left(\tfrac32-\tfrac13\right)=\tfrac{7}{27}\approx0.2593\).

(d) The count is \(\text{Bin}(5,\tfrac7{27})\): \(1-\left(\tfrac{20}{27}\right)^5-5\cdot\tfrac7{27}\left(\tfrac{20}{27}\right)^4\approx0.3867\).

8 points

A lamp stands 1 metre from a long straight wall. It is spun and stops pointing at an angle \(\Theta\), measured from the perpendicular to the wall, that is uniformly distributed on \((-\pi/2,\pi/2)\). Let \(X=\tan\Theta\) be the signed position (in metres) of the lit spot along the wall, measured from the point of the wall nearest the lamp.

  1. Find the CDF of \(X\).
  2. Find the density of \(X\).
  3. Find \(P\{|X|>1\}\) and \(P\{X>\sqrt3\}\).
  4. Show that \(E[|X|]\) is infinite.
Solution

(a) \(\tan\) is increasing on \((-\pi/2,\pi/2)\), so \(F_X(x)=P\{\Theta\le\arctan x\}=\dfrac{\arctan x+\pi/2}{\pi}=\tfrac12+\tfrac1\pi\arctan x\) for all real \(x\).

(b) \(f_X(x)=\dfrac{1}{\pi(1+x^2)}\), \(-\infty<x<\infty\).

(c) \(|X|>1\iff|\Theta|>\pi/4\), probability \(\tfrac{\pi/2}{\pi}=\tfrac12\). \(P\{X>\sqrt3\}=P\{\Theta>\pi/3\}=\tfrac{\pi/6}{\pi}=\tfrac16\).

(d) \(E[|X|]=2\int_0^\infty\dfrac{x}{\pi(1+x^2)}dx=\tfrac1\pi\big[\ln(1+x^2)\big]_0^\infty=\infty\). So \(X\) has no finite mean even though its density is symmetric about 0.

8 points

A basketball player makes each free throw with probability 0.75, independently. Over a season she attempts 300 free throws. Use a normal approximation with continuity correction throughout, after checking that one is reasonable.

  1. Check the condition for the normal approximation, and give the mean and standard deviation of the number of makes.
  2. Approximate the probability that she makes at least 240.
  3. Approximate the probability that she makes exactly 225.
  4. Approximate the probability that she makes between 215 and 235, inclusive.
Solution

(a) \(X\sim\text{Bin}(300,0.75)\). \(np(1-p)=56.25\ge10\). Mean \(225\), SD \(\sqrt{56.25}=7.5\).

(b) \(P\{X\ge240\}\approx P\{Z\ge\tfrac{239.5-225}{7.5}\}=P\{Z\ge1.93\}=1-0.9732=0.0268\). (Exact: 0.0246.)

(c) \(P\{224.5\le X\le225.5\}\approx P\{|Z|\le0.07\}=2(0.5279)-1=0.0558\). (Exact: 0.0531.)

(d) \(P\{214.5\le X\le235.5\}\approx P\{|Z|\le1.4\}=2(0.9192)-1=0.8384\).

9 points

On a given day in a dry town, there is no rain with probability 0.6. If it does rain, the amount of rain \(X\) (in mm) is exponentially distributed with mean 5 mm.

  1. Write down the CDF of \(X\), the day's rainfall (which is 0 on a dry day).
  2. Find the probability that more than 10 mm falls.
  3. Find \(E[X]\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\).
  4. The rain gauge only reads amounts above 2 mm, so a reading of “at most 2 mm” could be a dry day or a light rain. Given such a reading, find the probability that it rained.
Solution

(a) \(F(x)=0\) for \(x<0\); \(F(x)=0.6+0.4(1-e^{-x/5})=1-0.4e^{-x/5}\) for \(x\ge0\). There is a jump of 0.6 at 0.

(b) \(P\{X>10\}=0.4e^{-2}\approx0.0541\).

(c) Condition on rain: \(E[X]=0.4\cdot5=2\); \(E[X^2]=0.4\cdot(2\cdot5^2)=20\); \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=20-4=16\).

(d) \(P\{\text{rain}\mid X\le2\}=\dfrac{0.4(1-e^{-0.4})}{0.6+0.4(1-e^{-0.4})}=\dfrac{0.13187}{0.73187}\approx0.1802\).

9 points

A pump's lifetime \(T\) (in years) has hazard rate \(\lambda(t)=\dfrac{3t^2}{1000}\) for \(t>0\).

  1. Find the probability that a new pump lasts at least 5 years.
  2. Find the probability that a pump that is 5 years old lasts to age 10.
  3. Find the median lifetime.
  4. Find the density of \(T\).
Solution

Survival function: \(\bar F(t)=\exp\left(-\int_0^t\tfrac{3s^2}{1000}ds\right)=e^{-t^3/1000}\).

(a) \(e^{-125/1000}=e^{-0.125}\approx0.8825\).

(b) \(\dfrac{\bar F(10)}{\bar F(5)}=e^{-(1000-125)/1000}=e^{-0.875}\approx0.4169\). Compare with (a): an older pump is more likely to fail, as the rising hazard says.

(c) \(e^{-m^3/1000}=\tfrac12\Rightarrow m=(1000\ln2)^{1/3}\approx8.85\) years.

(d) \(f(t)=\lambda(t)\bar F(t)=\dfrac{3t^2}{1000}e^{-t^3/1000}\), \(t>0\).

Five students independently arrive for a 9:00 class at times uniformly distributed between 8:52 and 9:04.

  1. Find the probability that a given student is late.
  2. Find the probability that exactly two of the five are late.
  3. Find the probability that the last of the five arrives before 9:02.
  4. For one student, lateness is the number of minutes after 9:00 they arrive, or 0 if on time. Find the expected lateness.
Solution

Let \(U\) be minutes after 8:52, \(U\sim\text{Unif}(0,12)\).

(a) Late means \(U>8\): \(\tfrac4{12}=\tfrac13\).

(b) \(\binom52\left(\tfrac13\right)^2\left(\tfrac23\right)^3=\tfrac{80}{243}\approx0.3292\).

(c) All five must arrive before 9:02 (\(U<10\)): \(\left(\tfrac{10}{12}\right)^5\approx0.4019\).

(d) \(E[\max(U-8,0)]=\int_8^{12}(u-8)\tfrac1{12}du=\tfrac{8}{12}=\tfrac23\) minute.

9 points

A driver covers a 10-mile stretch of highway at a constant speed \(V\) (mph) that is uniformly distributed between 50 and 70. Let \(T=600/V\) be the time in minutes.

  1. Find the CDF of \(T\).
  2. Find the density of \(T\).
  3. Find \(E[T]\), and compare it with the time at the average speed.
  4. Find the probability that the trip takes more than 11 minutes.
Solution

\(T\) ranges from \(600/70=\tfrac{60}{7}\approx8.571\) to \(600/50=12\).

(a) For \(\tfrac{60}7\le t\le12\): \(P\{T\le t\}=P\{V\ge600/t\}=\dfrac{70-600/t}{20}\); 0 below and 1 above.

(b) \(f_T(t)=\dfrac{30}{t^2}\) for \(\tfrac{60}7<t<12\). (Same result from Theorem 5.7.1: \(f_V(v)=\tfrac1{20}\), \(v=600/t\), \(|dv/dt|=600/t^2\).)

(c) \(E[T]=\int_{50}^{70}\tfrac{600}{v}\cdot\tfrac1{20}dv=30\ln\tfrac75\approx10.0942\) minutes. At the mean speed 60 mph the trip takes 10 minutes, so \(E[600/V]\ne600/E[V]\).

(d) \(P\{V<600/11\}=\dfrac{600/11-50}{20}=\tfrac{5}{22}\approx0.2273\).

8 points

The size \(X\) (in MB) of a file stored on a server satisfies \(P\{X>x\}=x^{-3/2}\) for \(x\ge1\).

  1. Find the density of \(X\).
  2. Find \(E[X]\).
  3. Find the probability that a file larger than 2 MB is larger than 8 MB.
  4. Find the median file size, and show that \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\) is infinite.
Solution

(a) \(f(x)=\tfrac{d}{dx}\left(1-x^{-3/2}\right)=\tfrac32x^{-5/2}\), \(x\ge1\).

(b) \(E[X]=\int_1^\infty\tfrac32x^{-3/2}dx=\tfrac32\cdot2=3\) MB.

(c) \(\dfrac{P\{X>8\}}{P\{X>2\}}=\left(\tfrac28\right)^{3/2}=\tfrac18\).

(d) \(m^{-3/2}=\tfrac12\Rightarrow m=2^{2/3}\approx1.5874\) MB. \(E[X^2]=\int_1^\infty\tfrac32x^{-1/2}dx=\infty\), so the variance is infinite.
