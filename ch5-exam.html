The time a technician needs to repair a machine has density \(f(x)=\tfrac12e^{-x/2}\) for \(x\ge0\), with \(x\) in hours. The technician charges a flat $80 plus $50 per hour.

  1. Find the probability that a repair takes more than 3 hours, and the probability that it takes between 1 and 2 hours.
  2. A repair has already been going for 4 hours. Find the probability that it lasts at least 1 more hour.
  3. Find the mean and standard deviation of the charge.
  4. Find the probability that the charge exceeds $280.
Solution

\(X\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\tfrac12)\), so \(P\{X>x\}=e^{-x/2}\).

(a) \(e^{-1.5}\approx0.2231\). \(e^{-0.5}-e^{-1}\approx0.2387\).

(b) Memoryless: \(P\{X>5\mid X>4\}=P\{X>1\}=e^{-0.5}\approx0.6065\).

(c) \(E[X]=2\), \(\mathrm{SD}(X)=2\). Charge \(=80+50X\): mean \(\$180\), SD \(\$100\).

(d) \(80+50X>280\iff X>4\): \(e^{-2}\approx0.1353\).

9 points

\(X\) has density \(f(x)=x\) for \(0\le x\le1\), \(f(x)=2-x\) for \(1<x\le2\), and \(f(x)=0\) otherwise.

  1. Verify that \(f\) is a density.
  2. Find the CDF of \(X\).
  3. Find \(P\{\tfrac12<X<\tfrac32\}\).
  4. Find \(E[X]\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\).
Solution

(a) \(f\ge0\), and \(\int_0^1x\,dx+\int_1^2(2-x)\,dx=\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1\).

(b) \(F(x)=\tfrac{x^2}2\) for \(0\le x\le1\); \(F(x)=1-\tfrac{(2-x)^2}2\) for \(1<x\le2\); \(F=0\) below 0 and \(F=1\) above 2.

(c) \(F(\tfrac32)-F(\tfrac12)=\tfrac78-\tfrac18=\tfrac34\).

(d) \(E[X]=1\) by symmetry about 1. \(E[X^2]=\int_0^1x^3dx+\int_1^2x^2(2-x)\,dx=\tfrac14+\tfrac{11}{12}=\tfrac76\). \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=\tfrac76-1=\tfrac16\).

7 points

Scores on an aptitude test are normally distributed. Ten percent of test-takers score below 60, and 5 percent score above 90.

  1. Find the mean and standard deviation of the scores.
  2. Find the probability that a test-taker scores above the value \(\mu+\sigma\).
Solution

(a) \(\dfrac{60-\mu}\sigma=-1.28\) and \(\dfrac{90-\mu}\sigma=1.645\). Subtracting: \(\dfrac{30}\sigma=2.925\), so \(\sigma\approx10.26\) and \(\mu=60+1.28\sigma\approx73.13\).

(b) \(1-\Phi(1)=0.1587\), whatever \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are.

9 points

Let \(U\) be uniformly distributed on \((0,1)\).

  1. Find the CDF and density of \(Y=-2\ln U\). Name the distribution and give \(E[Y]\).
  2. Find the density of \(V=1/U\).
  3. Show that \(E[V]\) is infinite.
  4. Find \(E[U^3]\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(U^3)\) without finding the density of \(U^3\).
Solution

(a) For \(y>0\): \(P\{Y\le y\}=P\{U\ge e^{-y/2}\}=1-e^{-y/2}\), so \(f_Y(y)=\tfrac12e^{-y/2}\). \(Y\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\tfrac12)\), \(E[Y]=2\).

(b) For \(v>1\): \(P\{V\le v\}=P\{U\ge\tfrac1v\}=1-\tfrac1v\), so \(f_V(v)=\tfrac1{v^2}\) for \(v>1\), 0 otherwise.

(c) \(E[V]=\int_1^\infty v\cdot\tfrac1{v^2}dv=\int_1^\infty\tfrac1vdv=\infty\).

(d) \(E[U^3]=\int_0^1u^3du=\tfrac14\). \(E[U^6]=\tfrac17\). \(\mathrm{Var}(U^3)=\tfrac17-\tfrac1{16}=\tfrac9{112}\approx0.0804\).

8 points

The size \(X\) of an insurance claim, in thousands of dollars, has density \(f(x)=\dfrac{c}{x^3}\) for \(x\ge1\), and \(f(x)=0\) otherwise.

  1. Find \(c\).
  2. Find the CDF and \(P\{X>3\}\).
  3. Find the median claim size.
  4. Find \(E[X]\). What happens when you try to compute \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\)?
Solution

(a) \(\int_1^\infty cx^{-3}dx=\tfrac c2=1\Rightarrow c=2\).

(b) \(F(x)=1-\tfrac1{x^2}\) for \(x\ge1\), 0 otherwise. \(P\{X>3\}=\tfrac19\).

(c) \(1-\tfrac1{m^2}=\tfrac12\Rightarrow m=\sqrt2\approx1.414\), about $1,414.

(d) \(E[X]=\int_1^\infty2x^{-2}dx=2\). \(E[X^2]=\int_1^\infty\tfrac2xdx\) diverges, so the variance is infinite.

8 points

A call center takes 400 calls a day. Each call independently needs to be escalated to a supervisor with probability 0.2. The supervisors can handle at most 89 escalations a day.

  1. Approximate the probability that the supervisors are overwhelmed on a given day.
  2. Approximate the probability that the number of escalations is between 70 and 85, inclusive.
  3. State why the approximation is appropriate here.
Solution

\(S\sim\mathrm{Bin}(400,0.2)\): \(np=80\), \(np(1-p)=64\), \(\sigma=8\).

(a) \(P\{S\ge90\}=P\{S>89.5\}\approx1-\Phi\!\left(\tfrac{9.5}8\right)=1-\Phi(1.19)=0.1170\).

(b) \(P\{69.5<S<85.5\}\approx\Phi(0.69)-\Phi(-1.31)=0.7549-0.0951=0.6598\).

(c) \(np(1-p)=64\ge10\), so the normal approximation to the binomial is good; the half-unit shifts are the continuity correction.

7 points

A delivery is equally likely to arrive at any moment between 1:00 pm and 5:00 pm.

  1. Find the probability that it arrives after 4:00 pm.
  2. It has not arrived by 3:00 pm. Find the probability that it arrives before 3:30 pm.
  3. You start waiting at 1:00 pm, and each hour of waiting costs you $15. Find the mean and standard deviation of your cost.
Solution

Let \(W\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,4)\) be the hours after 1:00 pm.

(a) \(P\{W>3\}=\tfrac14\).

(b) \(\dfrac{P\{2<W<2.5\}}{P\{W>2\}}=\dfrac{0.5/4}{2/4}=\tfrac14\).

(c) \(E[W]=2\), \(\mathrm{Var}(W)=\tfrac{16}{12}\). Cost \(=15W\): mean \(\$30\), SD \(15\cdot\tfrac4{\sqrt{12}}\approx\$17.32\).

8 points

The lifetime \(X\) of a car, in years, has hazard rate function \(\lambda(t)=0.02+0.004t\) for \(t>0\).

  1. Find the probability that a new car lasts more than 5 years.
  2. Find the probability that a 5-year-old car lasts another 5 years.
  3. Find the density of \(X\).
  4. Is \(X\) memoryless? Explain.
Solution

\(\int_0^t\lambda(s)\,ds=0.02t+0.002t^2\), so \(P\{X>t\}=e^{-(0.02t+0.002t^2)}\).

(a) \(e^{-0.15}\approx0.8607\).

(b) \(\dfrac{P\{X>10\}}{P\{X>5\}}=\dfrac{e^{-0.40}}{e^{-0.15}}=e^{-0.25}\approx0.7788\).

(c) \(f(t)=(0.02+0.004t)\,e^{-(0.02t+0.002t^2)}\) for \(t>0\).

(d) No. The answers to (a) and (b) differ, and the hazard rate is not constant; an older car is more likely to fail.

9 points

The annual return on an investment fund, in percent, is normally distributed with mean 8 and standard deviation 12.

  1. Find the probability that the fund loses money in a year.
  2. Find the probability that the return is between 2 and 20 percent.
  3. Find the probability that the return exceeds 26 percent.
  4. Find the return that the fund beats in 95 percent of years.
Solution

(a) \(\Phi\!\left(\tfrac{0-8}{12}\right)=\Phi(-0.67)=1-0.7486=0.2514\).

(b) \(\Phi(1)-\Phi(-0.5)=0.8413-0.3085=0.5328\).

(c) \(1-\Phi(1.5)=0.0668\).

(d) The 5th percentile: \(8-1.645(12)=-11.74\) percent.

9 points

During a meteor shower, meteors are seen according to the three Poisson assumptions at a rate of 12 per hour.

  1. Find the probability that you wait more than 10 minutes for the first meteor.
  2. Name the distribution of the waiting time, in hours, until the first meteor, and give its mean in minutes.
  3. Find the probability of seeing exactly 3 meteors in 15 minutes.
  4. Find the probability that you wait more than 10 minutes for the second meteor.
Solution

\(N(t)\sim\mathrm{Po}(12t)\), \(t\) in hours.

(a) \(P\{N(\tfrac16)=0\}=e^{-2}\approx0.1353\).

(b) \(\mathrm{Exp}(12)\), mean \(\tfrac1{12}\) hour, which is 5 minutes.

(c) \(N(\tfrac14)\sim\mathrm{Po}(3)\): \(e^{-3}\tfrac{27}6\approx0.2240\).

(d) The second meteor takes more than 10 minutes iff at most 1 is seen in 10 minutes: \(e^{-2}(1+2)\approx0.4060\). The waiting time itself is \(\mathrm{Gamma}(2,12)\).

9 points

The CDF of \(X\) is \(F(x)=1-e^{-x^2}\) for \(x\ge0\), and \(F(x)=0\) for \(x<0\).

  1. Find the density of \(X\).
  2. Find \(P\{X>1\}\) and the median of \(X\).
  3. Find the hazard rate function of \(X\).
  4. Find \(P\{X>2\mid X>1\}\) and compare it with \(P\{X>1\}\).
Solution

(a) \(f(x)=F'(x)=2xe^{-x^2}\) for \(x\ge0\).

(b) \(P\{X>1\}=e^{-1}\approx0.3679\). Median: \(e^{-m^2}=\tfrac12\Rightarrow m=\sqrt{\ln2}\approx0.8326\).

(c) \(\lambda(t)=\dfrac{f(t)}{1-F(t)}=2t\).

(d) \(\dfrac{e^{-4}}{e^{-1}}=e^{-3}\approx0.0498\), far below \(0.3679\). The hazard rate increases, so the item wears out.

8 points

Let \(Z\) be a standard normal random variable and let \(Y=e^Z\).

  1. Find \(P\{Y>e\}\) and the median of \(Y\).
  2. Find the density of \(Y\).
  3. Find \(E[Y]\). Hint: complete the square in the exponent.
Solution

(a) \(P\{Y>e\}=P\{Z>1\}=0.1587\). \(P\{Y\le1\}=P\{Z\le0\}=\tfrac12\), so the median is 1.

(b) \(y=e^z\) is increasing with inverse \(z=\ln y\), \(\tfrac{dz}{dy}=\tfrac1y\). For \(y>0\): \(f_Y(y)=\dfrac1{y\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(\ln y)^2/2}\); 0 otherwise.

(c) \(E[e^Z]=\int_{-\infty}^\infty\tfrac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{z-z^2/2}dz=e^{1/2}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\tfrac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(z-1)^2/2}dz=e^{1/2}\approx1.6487\). The mean is above the median because the distribution is skewed right.
