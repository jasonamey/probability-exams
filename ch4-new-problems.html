1

A 12-question multiple-choice exam has 4 options per question. A student knows the answers to 6 questions and guesses at random on the other 6. Passing requires at least 9 correct.

  1. Find the probability the student passes.
  2. Find the mean and variance of the student's score.
Solution

(a) Let \(G\sim\mathrm{Bin}(6,\tfrac14)\) be the number of correct guesses. Passing needs \(G\ge3\): \(1-\left[(0.75)^6+6(0.25)(0.75)^5+15(0.25)^2(0.75)^4\right]=1-(0.1780+0.3560+0.2966)=0.1694\).

(b) Score \(=6+G\): mean \(6+1.5=7.5\); variance \(6(0.25)(0.75)=1.125\).

2

A bakery mixes 600 chocolate chips into dough that is divided into 200 cookies. Each chip is equally likely to land in any cookie, independently of the others.

  1. State the exact distribution of the number of chips in a given cookie, and a suitable approximation.
  2. Approximate the probability that a given cookie has no chips.
  3. Treating cookies as independent, approximate the probability that a box of 12 contains at least one chipless cookie.
  4. How many chips are needed so that at most 1 percent of cookies have none?
Solution

(a) Exactly \(\mathrm{Bin}(600,\tfrac1{200})\); approximately \(\mathrm{Po}(3)\), since \(n\) is large, \(p\) is small, and \(np=3\).

(b) \(e^{-3}\approx0.0498\).

(c) \(1-(1-e^{-3})^{12}\approx0.4582\).

(d) With \(n\) chips, \(\lambda=\tfrac n{200}\). Need \(e^{-n/200}\le0.01\Rightarrow n\ge200\ln100\approx921.03\), so 922 chips.

3

A drawer holds a fair coin and a biased coin that lands heads with probability 0.8. One coin is chosen at random and flipped 5 times, giving exactly 4 heads.

  1. Find the probability that the biased coin was chosen.
  2. Find the probability that a sixth flip of the same coin lands heads.
Solution

(a) Likelihoods: biased \(5(0.8)^4(0.2)=0.4096\); fair \(5(0.5)^5=0.15625\). Equal priors cancel: \(\dfrac{0.4096}{0.4096+0.15625}\approx0.7239\).

(b) \(0.7239(0.8)+0.2761(0.5)\approx0.7172\).

4

Two teams play a best-of-seven series; the first to win 4 games takes it. Team A wins each game independently with probability 0.6.

  1. Find the probability that A wins the series in exactly 6 games.
  2. Find the probability that A wins the series.
  3. Find the probability that the series goes to 7 games.
Solution

(a) A's 4th win on game 6: \(\binom53(0.6)^4(0.4)^2=10(0.1296)(0.16)\approx0.2074\).

(b) \(\sum_{n=4}^7\binom{n-1}3(0.6)^4(0.4)^{n-4}=0.1296\,(1+1.6+1.6+1.28)\approx0.7102\).

(c) The first 6 games split 3–3: \(\binom63(0.6)^3(0.4)^3=20(0.216)(0.064)\approx0.2765\).

5

A newsstand buys papers for $1.00 each and sells them for $2.50. Unsold papers are worthless. Daily demand \(D\) has this PMF:

d0123
P{D = d}0.10.30.40.2
  1. If the stand stocks \(s\) papers, write the profit as a function of \(D\).
  2. Find the expected profit for \(s=1\), \(s=2\), and \(s=3\). How many papers should it stock?
Solution

(a) Profit \(=2.5\min(D,s)-s\).

(b) \(E[\min(D,1)]=0.9\), \(E[\min(D,2)]=0.3+2(0.6)=1.5\), \(E[\min(D,3)]=E[D]=1.7\). Expected profits: \(s=1\): \(2.25-1=\$1.25\); \(s=2\): \(3.75-2=\$1.75\); \(s=3\): \(4.25-3=\$1.25\). Stock 2.

6

A fair die is rolled repeatedly.

  1. Let \(X\) be the number of rolls until the first 6. Find \(P\{X>6\}\) and the probability that \(X\) is even.
  2. Let \(T\) be the number of rolls until all six faces have appeared at least once. Find \(E[T]\). Hint: write \(T\) as a sum of waiting times for each new face.
Solution

(a) \(P\{X>6\}=(\tfrac56)^6\approx0.3349\). With \(q=\tfrac56\), \(p=\tfrac16\): \(P(\text{even})=\sum_{k\ge1}q^{2k-1}p=\dfrac{qp}{1-q^2}=\dfrac q{1+q}=\dfrac5{11}\).

(b) After \(i\) distinct faces have appeared, the wait for a new one is geometric with \(p=\tfrac{6-i}6\) and mean \(\tfrac6{6-i}\). \(E[T]=6\left(\tfrac16+\tfrac15+\tfrac14+\tfrac13+\tfrac12+1\right)=14.7\).

7

A system works if a majority of its components work. Components work independently, each with probability \(p\).

  1. With \(p=0.9\), find the probability that a 5-component system works, and that a 3-component system works. Which is more reliable?
  2. Repeat with \(p=0.4\).
  3. Explain in one sentence why the answer flips.
Solution

(a) Five: \(\binom53(0.9)^3(0.1)^2+\binom54(0.9)^4(0.1)+(0.9)^5=0.0729+0.3281+0.5905=0.9914\). Three: \(3(0.9)^2(0.1)+(0.9)^3=0.972\). Five is better.

(b) Five: \(0.2304+0.0768+0.0102=0.3174\). Three: \(3(0.4)^2(0.6)+(0.4)^3=0.352\). Three is better.

(c) More components make the working fraction concentrate near \(p\), which helps when \(p>\tfrac12\) and hurts when \(p<\tfrac12\).

8

In its first year a laptop needs a full replacement costing $900 with probability 0.06, needs a $200 repair with probability 0.10, and otherwise costs nothing. A one-year warranty that covers everything costs $95.

  1. Find the expected out-of-pocket cost without the warranty. Is the warranty a good deal on average?
  2. Find the standard deviation of the cost without the warranty.
  3. At what replacement probability would the warranty break even, keeping the repair probability at 0.10?
Solution

(a) \(E[C]=900(0.06)+200(0.10)=\$74<\$95\). On average the warranty costs $21 more than it saves.

(b) \(E[C^2]=810000(0.06)+40000(0.10)=52600\). \(\mathrm{Var}(C)=52600-74^2=47124\). \(\mathrm{SD}(C)\approx\$217.08\).

(c) \(900q+20=95\Rightarrow q=\tfrac{75}{900}\approx0.0833\).

9

Ten people board an elevator on the ground floor of a building with 6 upper floors. Each person independently gets off at a uniformly chosen upper floor. Let \(X\) be the number of floors at which the elevator stops.

  1. Find \(E[X]\).
  2. Find \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\). You will need the probability that the elevator stops at both of two given floors.
Solution

(a) Let \(I_j\) indicate a stop at floor \(j\). \(P(I_j=1)=1-(\tfrac56)^{10}\approx0.8385\). \(E[X]=6(0.8385)\approx5.031\).

(b) For \(i\ne j\), by inclusion–exclusion on "no stop at \(i\)" and "no stop at \(j\)": \(P(I_i=I_j=1)=1-2(\tfrac56)^{10}+(\tfrac46)^{10}\approx0.6943\). \(E[X^2]=6(0.8385)+30(0.6943)\approx25.861\). \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\approx25.861-5.031^2\approx0.550\).

10

Two fair four-sided dice, each numbered 1 through 4, are rolled. Let \(X\) be the larger of the two numbers (or the common value if they match).

  1. Find the CDF of \(X\) at \(x=1,2,3,4\). Start from the CDF, not the PMF.
  2. Use the CDF to get the PMF.
  3. Find \(E[X]\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\).
Solution

(a) \(X\le k\) iff both dice are at most \(k\): \(F(k)=\left(\tfrac k4\right)^2=\tfrac{k^2}{16}\), so \(\tfrac1{16},\tfrac4{16},\tfrac9{16},1\).

(b) \(p(k)=F(k)-F(k-1)=\tfrac{2k-1}{16}\): \(\tfrac1{16},\tfrac3{16},\tfrac5{16},\tfrac7{16}\).

(c) \(E[X]=\tfrac{1+6+15+28}{16}=\tfrac{25}8=3.125\). \(E[X^2]=\tfrac{1+12+45+112}{16}=\tfrac{85}8\). \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=\tfrac{85}8-\tfrac{625}{64}=\tfrac{55}{64}\approx0.8594\).

11

A random variable \(X\) has \(E[X]=3\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=4\). Nothing else is known about its distribution.

  1. Find \(E[X^2]\) and \(E[(2X-1)^2]\).
  2. Find \(\mathrm{Var}(7-3X)\) and \(\mathrm{SD}(7-3X)\).
  3. Show that \(E[(X-c)^2]\) is smallest when \(c=E[X]\), and give the minimum value.
Solution

(a) \(E[X^2]=\mathrm{Var}(X)+(E[X])^2=13\). \(E[4X^2-4X+1]=52-12+1=41\).

(b) \(\mathrm{Var}(7-3X)=9(4)=36\); SD \(=6\).

(c) \(E[(X-c)^2]=E[X^2]-2cE[X]+c^2=13-6c+c^2=(c-3)^2+4\), minimized at \(c=3\) with value \(4=\mathrm{Var}(X)\).

12

\(X\) is a Poisson random variable with \(P\{X=1\}=P\{X=2\}\).

  1. Find \(\lambda\).
  2. Find \(P\{X=4\}\) and \(P\{X\ge3\}\).
  3. Find \(E[X^2]\).
  4. Find the probability that \(X\) is even. Hint: add the series for \(e^{\lambda}\) and \(e^{-\lambda}\).
Solution

(a) \(\lambda e^{-\lambda}=\tfrac{\lambda^2}2e^{-\lambda}\Rightarrow\lambda=2\).

(b) \(P\{X=4\}=e^{-2}\tfrac{16}{24}\approx0.0902\). \(P\{X\ge3\}=1-e^{-2}(1+2+2)\approx0.3233\).

(c) \(E[X^2]=\lambda+\lambda^2=6\).

(d) \(\sum_{k\text{ even}}\tfrac{\lambda^k}{k!}=\tfrac{e^{\lambda}+e^{-\lambda}}2\), so \(P(\text{even})=\tfrac{1+e^{-2\lambda}}2=\tfrac{1+e^{-4}}2\approx0.5092\).

13

A random variable \(X\) has PMF \(p(k)=\dfrac{c}{k(k+1)}\) for \(k=1,2,3,\dots\)

  1. Find \(c\). Hint: \(\tfrac1{k(k+1)}=\tfrac1k-\tfrac1{k+1}\).
  2. Find \(P\{X>n\}\) for a positive integer \(n\), then \(P\{3\le X\le6\}\).
  3. Show that \(E[X]\) is infinite.
Solution

(a) The sum telescopes: \(\sum_{k\ge1}\left(\tfrac1k-\tfrac1{k+1}\right)=1\), so \(c=1\).

(b) \(P\{X>n\}=\sum_{k>n}\left(\tfrac1k-\tfrac1{k+1}\right)=\tfrac1{n+1}\). \(P\{3\le X\le6\}=P\{X>2\}-P\{X>6\}=\tfrac13-\tfrac17=\tfrac4{21}\).

(c) \(E[X]=\sum_{k\ge1}k\cdot\tfrac1{k(k+1)}=\sum_{k\ge1}\tfrac1{k+1}\), the harmonic series, which diverges.

14

Calls reach a help desk according to the three Poisson assumptions at a rate of 6 per hour.

  1. Find the probability of no calls in a 10-minute break.
  2. Given that exactly 2 calls arrived in a particular hour, find the probability that both arrived in the first 20 minutes.
Solution

(a) \(N(\tfrac16)\sim\mathrm{Po}(1)\): \(e^{-1}\approx0.3679\).

(b) Counts in the first 20 minutes and last 40 minutes are independent \(\mathrm{Po}(2)\) and \(\mathrm{Po}(4)\):

$$\frac{P\{\mathrm{Po}(2)=2\}\,P\{\mathrm{Po}(4)=0\}}{P\{\mathrm{Po}(6)=2\}}=\frac{2e^{-2}\cdot e^{-4}}{18e^{-6}}=\frac19.$$

This equals \((\tfrac13)^2\): given the count, each call falls in the first third of the hour with probability \(\tfrac13\).

15

A lake holds 500 fish, 50 of which were tagged last month. A biologist now nets 40 fish at random without replacement; \(X\) is the number of tagged fish in the net.

  1. Write the exact expression for \(P\{X=4\}\), then approximate it with a binomial.
  2. Find \(E[X]\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\).
  3. Suppose the lake's size \(N\) were unknown and the net held 5 tagged fish. Estimate \(N\) by matching the observed count to \(E[X]\).
Solution

(a) \(\dfrac{\binom{50}4\binom{450}{36}}{\binom{500}{40}}\approx0.2146\). Binomial \((40,0.1)\): \(\binom{40}4(0.1)^4(0.9)^{36}\approx0.2059\).

(b) \(E[X]=40\cdot\tfrac{50}{500}=4\). \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=40(0.1)(0.9)\cdot\tfrac{460}{499}\approx3.3186\).

(c) \(40\cdot\tfrac{50}N=5\Rightarrow N=400\).
