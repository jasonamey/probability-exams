A gambler bets $1 on red at roulette 1000 times. Each bet wins $1 with probability \(\tfrac{18}{38}\) and loses $1 otherwise, independently.

  1. Find the gambler's expected net winnings.
  2. Approximate the probability that the gambler is ahead after the 1000 bets. Justify the approximation you use.
  3. Approximate the probability that the gambler is down $100 or more.
Solution

Let \(X\) be the number of wins, \(X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(1000,\tfrac{18}{38})\). Net winnings are \(X-(1000-X)=2X-1000\).

(a) \(E[X]=473.68\), so \(E[2X-1000]=-\$52.63\).

(b) \(np(1-p)=249.3\ge10\), so the normal approximation is appropriate, with \(\mu=473.68\) and \(\sigma=\sqrt{249.3}=15.79\). Ahead means \(2X-1000>0\), i.e. \(X\ge501\). With the continuity correction, \(P\{X\ge500.5\}\approx1-\Phi\!\left(\tfrac{500.5-473.68}{15.79}\right)=1-\Phi(1.70)=0.0446\).

(c) \(2X-1000\le-100\iff X\le450\). \(P\{X\le450.5\}\approx\Phi\!\left(\tfrac{450.5-473.68}{15.79}\right)=\Phi(-1.47)=1-0.9292=0.0708\).

9 points

A random variable \(X\) has CDF \(F(x)=\dfrac{x}{1+x}\) for \(x\ge0\) and \(F(x)=0\) for \(x<0\).

  1. Find the density of \(X\), its median, and \(P\{X>3\}\).
  2. Find \(P\{X>5\mid X>2\}\). Does \(X\) have the memoryless property?
  3. Determine whether \(E[X]\) is finite.
  4. Find the CDF of \(Y=1/X\). What do you notice?
Solution

(a) \(f(x)=F'(x)=\dfrac1{(1+x)^2}\) for \(x>0\). Median: \(\tfrac m{1+m}=\tfrac12\Rightarrow m=1\). \(P\{X>3\}=1-\tfrac34=\tfrac14\).

(b) \(P\{X>x\}=\tfrac1{1+x}\), so \(P\{X>5\mid X>2\}=\dfrac{1/6}{1/3}=\tfrac12\). Memorylessness would require this to equal \(P\{X>3\}=\tfrac14\). It does not, so \(X\) is not memoryless.

(c) \(E[X]=\int_0^\infty\dfrac{x}{(1+x)^2}\,dx\). With \(u=1+x\): \(\int_1^\infty\left(\tfrac1u-\tfrac1{u^2}\right)du=\left[\ln u+\tfrac1u\right]_1^\infty=\infty\). The mean is infinite.

(d) For \(y>0\): \(F_Y(y)=P\{1/X\le y\}=P\{X\ge1/y\}=\dfrac1{1+1/y}=\dfrac y{1+y}\). \(Y\) has the same distribution as \(X\).

A 12-inch stick is broken at a point chosen uniformly at random along its length.

  1. Find the probability that the shorter piece is less than 2 inches long.
  2. Find the probability that the longer piece is at least twice as long as the shorter piece.
  3. Find the expected length of the longer piece.
  4. The two pieces are used as the sides of a rectangle. Find the expected area of the rectangle.
Solution

Let \(X\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,12)\) be the break point; the pieces are \(X\) and \(12-X\).

(a) \(X<2\) or \(X>10\): \(\tfrac4{12}=\tfrac13\).

(b) If \(X\) is the shorter piece, need \(12-X\ge2X\), i.e. \(X\le4\). By symmetry the event is \(X\le4\) or \(X\ge8\): \(\tfrac8{12}=\tfrac23\).

(c) The longer piece is \(\max(X,12-X)\). \(E=\int_0^6\tfrac{12-x}{12}dx+\int_6^{12}\tfrac x{12}dx=4.5+4.5=9\) inches. (Equivalently, the longer piece is uniform on \((6,12)\).)

(d) \(E[X(12-X)]=12E[X]-E[X^2]=72-\int_0^{12}\tfrac{x^2}{12}dx=72-48=24\) square inches.

A warehouse stocks light bulbs from two suppliers. Forty percent come from supplier A, whose bulbs have exponentially distributed lifetimes with mean 1000 hours; the rest come from supplier B, whose bulbs have exponentially distributed lifetimes with mean 2000 hours. A bulb is picked at random and switched on.

  1. Find the probability that the bulb is still working after 1500 hours.
  2. Given that it is still working after 1500 hours, find the probability that it came from supplier A.
  3. Given that it is still working after 1500 hours, find the probability that it works for at least 1500 more hours. Is the lifetime of a randomly picked bulb memoryless?
Solution

Let \(T\) be the lifetime.

(a) \(P\{T>1500\}=0.4e^{-1.5}+0.6e^{-0.75}=0.0893+0.2834=0.3727\).

(b) \(P(A\mid T>1500)=\dfrac{0.4e^{-1.5}}{0.3727}=0.2395\). Surviving bulbs are more likely to be from B.

(c) \(P\{T>3000\mid T>1500\}=\dfrac{0.4e^{-3}+0.6e^{-1.5}}{0.3727}=\dfrac{0.1538}{0.3727}=0.4127\). This is not \(P\{T>1500\}=0.3727\), so \(T\) is not memoryless, even though each supplier's bulbs are. A mixture of exponentials is not exponential.

9 points

The time \(X\), in hours, that a customer spends in a store has density \(f(x)=c\,x\,e^{-2x}\) for \(x>0\), and \(f(x)=0\) otherwise.

  1. Find \(c\).
  2. Find the probability that a customer stays more than 1 hour.
  3. Find \(E[X]\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\).
  4. The amount a customer spends is \(10e^{X}\) dollars. Find the expected amount spent.
Solution

(a) \(\int_0^\infty xe^{-2x}dx=\tfrac{1!}{2^2}=\tfrac14\), so \(c=4\).

(b) By parts, \(\int_1^\infty4xe^{-2x}dx=\left[-2xe^{-2x}-e^{-2x}\right]_1^\infty=3e^{-2}\approx0.4060\).

(c) \(E[X]=4\int_0^\infty x^2e^{-2x}dx=4\cdot\tfrac{2}{8}=1\). \(E[X^2]=4\int_0^\infty x^3e^{-2x}dx=4\cdot\tfrac{6}{16}=\tfrac32\). \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=\tfrac32-1=\tfrac12\).

(d) \(E[10e^X]=10\int_0^\infty e^x\cdot4xe^{-2x}dx=40\int_0^\infty xe^{-x}dx=40\). Note this is not \(10e^{E[X]}=27.18\).

9 points

A machine produces shafts whose diameters are normally distributed with mean 10.1 mm and standard deviation 0.15 mm. A shaft is acceptable if its diameter is between 9.7 mm and 10.3 mm.

  1. Find the probability that a shaft is acceptable.
  2. Five shafts are produced independently. Find the probability that at least four are acceptable.
  3. The machine is recalibrated so that the mean is 10.0 mm, with the same standard deviation. Find the new probability that a shaft is acceptable.
  4. With the mean at 10.0 mm, how small must the standard deviation be for 99% of shafts to be acceptable?
Solution

(a) \(\Phi\!\left(\tfrac{10.3-10.1}{0.15}\right)-\Phi\!\left(\tfrac{9.7-10.1}{0.15}\right)=\Phi(1.33)-\Phi(-2.67)=0.9082-0.0038=0.9044\).

(b) The number acceptable is \(\mathrm{Bin}(5,0.9044)\): \(5(0.9044)^4(0.0956)+(0.9044)^5=0.3198+0.6051=0.9249\).

(c) \(\Phi(2)-\Phi(-2)=2(0.9772)-1=0.9544\).

(d) Need \(2\Phi(0.3/\sigma)-1=0.99\), so \(\Phi(0.3/\sigma)=0.995\), \(0.3/\sigma=2.576\), \(\sigma\le0.1165\) mm.

9 points

\(X\) is uniformly distributed on \((-1,2)\), and \(Y=X^2\).

  1. Find the CDF of \(Y\).
  2. Find the density of \(Y\).
  3. Find \(P\{Y>\tfrac14\}\) and \(E[Y]\).
Solution

\(Y\) takes values in \([0,4)\), and \(x\mapsto x^2\) is not monotone on \((-1,2)\), so work from the CDF.

(a) For \(0\le y\le1\): \(F_Y(y)=P\{-\sqrt y\le X\le\sqrt y\}=\dfrac{2\sqrt y}3\). For \(1<y<4\): the constraint \(X\ge-\sqrt y\) always holds, so \(F_Y(y)=P\{X\le\sqrt y\}=\dfrac{\sqrt y+1}3\). \(F_Y=0\) for \(y<0\) and \(F_Y=1\) for \(y\ge4\). (Check: both pieces give \(\tfrac23\) at \(y=1\).)

(b) \(f_Y(y)=\dfrac1{3\sqrt y}\) for \(0<y<1\), \(f_Y(y)=\dfrac1{6\sqrt y}\) for \(1<y<4\), and 0 otherwise.

(c) \(P\{Y>\tfrac14\}=1-\tfrac{2(1/2)}3=\tfrac23\). \(E[Y]=E[X^2]=\int_{-1}^2\tfrac{x^2}3dx=\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac{8+1}3=1\).

The length of a phone call is exponentially distributed with mean 2 minutes. The phone company bills in whole minutes, rounding every call up: a call of 2.3 minutes is billed as 3 minutes. Let \(N\) be the number of minutes billed.

  1. Find \(P\{N=k\}\) for \(k=1,2,3,\dots\) and identify the distribution of \(N\).
  2. Find \(E[N]\). On average, how many minutes per call are billed but not used?
  3. Find the probability that a call is billed for 4 or more minutes.
Solution

Let \(X\) be the call length, \(P\{X>x\}=e^{-x/2}\).

(a) \(P\{N=k\}=P\{k-1<X\le k\}=e^{-(k-1)/2}-e^{-k/2}=\left(e^{-1/2}\right)^{k-1}\left(1-e^{-1/2}\right)\). This is geometric with \(p=1-e^{-1/2}\approx0.3935\).

(b) \(E[N]=\dfrac1p=\dfrac1{1-e^{-1/2}}\approx2.541\) minutes. Since \(E[X]=2\), about 0.541 minutes per call are billed but not used.

(c) \(P\{N\ge4\}=P\{X>3\}=e^{-1.5}\approx0.2231\).

The lifetime \(T\), in years, of an electronic component has hazard rate \(\lambda(t)=\dfrac{2}{t+5}\) for \(t\ge0\).

  1. Find \(P\{T>t\}\).
  2. Compare the probability that a new component lasts 10 years with the probability that a 5-year-old working component lasts 10 more years. Explain the comparison in terms of the hazard rate.
  3. Find the median lifetime.
  4. Find the mean lifetime.
Solution

(a) \(P\{T>t\}=\exp\!\left(-\int_0^t\tfrac2{s+5}ds\right)=\exp\!\left(-2\ln\tfrac{t+5}5\right)=\left(\dfrac5{t+5}\right)^2\).

(b) New: \(\left(\tfrac5{15}\right)^2=\tfrac19\). Used: \(\dfrac{P\{T>15\}}{P\{T>5\}}=\dfrac{1/16}{1/4}=\tfrac14\). The used component is more likely to last, because the hazard rate is decreasing: the longer a component has survived, the lower its instantaneous failure rate.

(c) \(\left(\tfrac5{m+5}\right)^2=\tfrac12\Rightarrow m=5(\sqrt2-1)\approx2.07\) years.

(d) The density is \(f(t)=-\tfrac d{dt}P\{T>t\}=\dfrac{50}{(t+5)^3}\). With \(u=t+5\): \(E[T]=\int_5^\infty\dfrac{50(u-5)}{u^3}du=50\left[-\tfrac1u+\tfrac5{2u^2}\right]_5^\infty=50\left(\tfrac15-\tfrac1{10}\right)=5\) years.

A dart is aimed at a vertical line on a board. The horizontal miss \(X\), in cm, is normally distributed with mean 0 and standard deviation 2; negative values are misses to the left.

  1. Find the probability that the dart lands within 1 cm of the line.
  2. Find the distance \(d\) such that 90% of darts land within \(d\) cm of the line.
  3. Find the expected distance \(E[|X|]\) between the dart and the line.
  4. Find \(\mathrm{Var}(|X|)\).
Solution

(a) \(P\{|X|\le1\}=2\Phi(0.5)-1=0.3830\).

(b) \(2\Phi(d/2)-1=0.90\Rightarrow\Phi(d/2)=0.95\Rightarrow d=2(1.645)=3.29\) cm.

(c) By symmetry, \(E[|X|]=2\int_0^\infty x\,\dfrac1{2\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/8}dx=\dfrac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left[-4e^{-x^2/8}\right]_0^\infty=\dfrac4{\sqrt{2\pi}}=2\sqrt{\tfrac2\pi}\approx1.596\) cm.

(d) \(E[|X|^2]=E[X^2]=\sigma^2=4\). \(\mathrm{Var}(|X|)=4-\tfrac8\pi\approx1.454\).

A gas station's weekly demand for premium fuel is uniformly distributed between 0 and 1000 gallons. At the start of the week the owner buys \(q\) gallons at $1 per gallon and sells at $3 per gallon. Fuel left at the end of the week is worthless, and demand beyond \(q\) is lost.

  1. Find the expected profit if \(q=500\).
  2. Find the expected profit as a function of \(q\), for \(0\le q\le1000\), and the value of \(q\) that maximizes it.
  3. At the optimal \(q\), find the probability that the station sells out.
Solution

Let \(X\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1000)\). Gallons sold are \(\min(X,q)\), so profit is \(3\min(X,q)-q\).

\(E[\min(X,q)]=\int_0^q\tfrac x{1000}dx+q\,P\{X>q\}=\tfrac{q^2}{2000}+q\left(1-\tfrac q{1000}\right)=q-\tfrac{q^2}{2000}\).

(a) \(3(500-125)-500=\$625\).

(b) \(g(q)=3\left(q-\tfrac{q^2}{2000}\right)-q=2q-\tfrac{3q^2}{2000}\). \(g'(q)=2-\tfrac{3q}{1000}=0\Rightarrow q=\tfrac{2000}3\approx667\) gallons, a maximum since \(g''<0\). The maximum expected profit is \(g(\tfrac{2000}3)\approx\$666.67\).

(c) \(P\{X>\tfrac{2000}3\}=\tfrac13\).

A device contains three components that fail independently. Each has an exponentially distributed lifetime with mean 6 years.

  1. Suppose the device works only while all three components work. Find the probability that it works for more than 2 years.
  2. In the setting of part (a), find the distribution of the device's lifetime and its mean.
  3. Suppose instead the device works as long as at least one component works. Find the probability that it fails within 3 years.
  4. In the setting of part (c), find the median lifetime of the device.
Solution

Each component has \(P\{T_i>t\}=e^{-t/6}\).

(a) \(P\{\text{all}>2\}=\left(e^{-2/6}\right)^3=e^{-1}\approx0.3679\).

(b) \(P\{\min>t\}=\left(e^{-t/6}\right)^3=e^{-t/2}\): exponential with rate \(\tfrac12\), mean 2 years.

(c) \(P\{\max\le3\}=\left(1-e^{-1/2}\right)^3=(0.3935)^3\approx0.0609\).

(d) \(\left(1-e^{-m/6}\right)^3=\tfrac12\Rightarrow e^{-m/6}=1-2^{-1/3}=0.2063\Rightarrow m=-6\ln(0.2063)\approx9.47\) years.
