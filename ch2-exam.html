8 points

Three fair dice are rolled.

  1. Find the probability that all three show different numbers.
  2. Find the probability that the sum is 10.
  3. Find the probability that the largest number showing is 4.
  4. Find the probability that the first die is less than the second, which is less than the third.
Solution

The \(6^3=216\) ordered outcomes are equally likely.

(a) \(6\cdot5\cdot4=120\) outcomes, so \(120/216=5/9\).

(b) Count ordered triples with sum 10. Unordered patterns: \(\{6,3,1\},\{6,2,2\},\{5,4,1\},\{5,3,2\},\{4,4,2\},\{4,3,3\}\) give \(6+3+6+6+3+3=27\) ordered outcomes, so \(27/216=1/8\).

(c) \(P\{\max\le4\}-P\{\max\le3\}=\dfrac{4^3-3^3}{216}=\dfrac{37}{216}\).

(d) Each set of 3 distinct values can be listed increasingly in one way: \(\binom63/216=20/216=5/54\).

9 points

In a survey of households, 55% subscribe to streaming service N, 30% to service H and 25% to service D. Also 15% subscribe to both N and H, 12% to both N and D, and 8% to both H and D. Altogether 79% subscribe to at least one of the three.

  1. Find the probability that a randomly chosen household subscribes to all three.
  2. Find the probability that it subscribes to exactly one of the three.
  3. Find the probability that it subscribes to exactly two of the three.
  4. Find the probability that it subscribes to N or H but not to D.
Solution

(a) Inclusion–exclusion: \(0.79=0.55+0.30+0.25-0.15-0.12-0.08+P(NHD)\), so \(P(NHD)=0.79-0.75=0.04\).

(b) \(P(\text{exactly one})=\sum P(E_i)-2\sum P(E_iE_j)+3P(NHD)=1.10-0.70+0.12=0.52\).

(c) \(P(\text{exactly two})=\sum P(E_iE_j)-3P(NHD)=0.35-0.12=0.23\). (Check: \(0.21+0.52+0.23+0.04=1\).)

(d) \(P\big((N\cup H)D^c\big)=P(N\cup H)-P\big((N\cup H)D\big)\). Here \(P(N\cup H)=0.55+0.30-0.15=0.70\) and \(P(ND\cup HD)=0.12+0.08-0.04=0.16\). Answer \(0.70-0.16=0.54\).

8 points

Eight students, 4 chemistry majors and 4 biology majors, are paired at random into 4 lab pairs, with every pairing equally likely. Sam and Tia are two of the students; Sam is a chemistry major and Tia a biology major.

  1. How many pairings are possible?
  2. Find the probability that every pair contains one chemistry and one biology major.
  3. Find the probability that Sam and Tia are lab partners.
  4. Find the probability that the chemistry majors are all paired with each other (so the biology majors are too).
Solution

(a) \(\dfrac{8!}{(2!)^4\,4!}=105\).

(b) Line up the chemistry majors; the biology majors can be matched to them in \(4!=24\) ways: \(24/105=8/35\).

(c) Sam's partner is equally likely to be any of the other 7 students: \(1/7\). (Or: pairings of the other 6 number \(15\), and \(15/105=1/7\).)

(d) Pair the 4 chemistry majors (3 ways) and the 4 biology majors (3 ways): \(9/105=3/35\).

8 points

In a lottery, 6 numbers are drawn at random without replacement from 1 to 45. A player picks 6 numbers in advance.

  1. Find the probability that the player matches all 6.
  2. Find the probability that the player matches exactly 4.
  3. Find the probability that the player matches at least one.
  4. Find the probability that the smallest number drawn is 10.
Solution

There are \(\binom{45}6=8{,}145{,}060\) equally likely draws.

(a) \(1/8{,}145{,}060\approx1.23\times10^{-7}\).

(b) Choose 4 of the player's 6 and 2 of the other 39: \(\dfrac{\binom64\binom{39}2}{\binom{45}6}=\dfrac{15\cdot741}{8{,}145{,}060}=\dfrac{11{,}115}{8{,}145{,}060}\approx0.0014\).

(c) Complement: all 6 drawn from the 39 numbers the player did not pick. \(1-\dfrac{\binom{39}6}{\binom{45}6}=1-\dfrac{3{,}262{,}623}{8{,}145{,}060}\approx1-0.4006=0.5994\).

(d) 10 is drawn and the other 5 come from 11–45: \(\dfrac{\binom{35}5}{\binom{45}6}=\dfrac{324{,}632}{8{,}145{,}060}\approx0.0399\).

8 points

At a company, 60% of employees use the gym and 70% use the cafeteria. Let \(A\) be the event that a randomly chosen employee uses the gym and \(B\) the event that the employee uses the cafeteria.

  1. Using only the axioms and their simple consequences, find the smallest and largest possible values of \(P(AB)\).
  2. Find the smallest and largest possible values of \(P(A\cup B)\).
  3. Suppose in addition that 10% of employees use neither. Find \(P(AB)\).
  4. Under the assumption in (c), find the probability that an employee uses exactly one of the two, and the probability that an employee uses the gym but not the cafeteria.
Solution

(a) \(P(AB)\le\min\{P(A),P(B)\}=0.6\), attained when \(A\subset B\). Also \(P(AB)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cup B)\ge1.3-1=0.3\), attained when \(A\cup B=S\). So \(0.3\le P(AB)\le0.6\).

(b) \(P(A\cup B)\ge\max\{P(A),P(B)\}=0.7\) and \(P(A\cup B)\le1\); both are attainable (the cases in (a)). So \(0.7\le P(A\cup B)\le1\).

(c) \(P(A\cup B)=1-P(A^cB^c)=0.9\), so \(P(AB)=0.6+0.7-0.9=0.4\).

(d) Exactly one: \(P(A\cup B)-P(AB)=0.9-0.4=0.5\). Gym but not cafeteria: \(P(AB^c)=P(A)-P(AB)=0.6-0.4=0.2\).

8 points

A fair coin is tossed 10 times.

  1. Find the probability of exactly 4 heads.
  2. Find the probability of at least 2 heads.
  3. Find the probability that no two heads occur on consecutive tosses.
  4. Find the probability that the first head appears on the fourth toss or later.
Solution

All \(2^{10}=1024\) sequences are equally likely.

(a) \(\binom{10}4/1024=210/1024=105/512\approx0.2051\).

(b) Complement of 0 or 1 head: \(1-\dfrac{1+10}{1024}=\dfrac{1013}{1024}\approx0.9893\).

(c) With \(k\) heads and \(10-k\) tails, place the tails first; the heads must go into distinct gaps among the \(11-k\) gaps, in \(\binom{11-k}{k}\) ways. Summing over \(k=0,\dots,5\): \(1+10+36+56+35+6=144\). Probability \(144/1024=9/64\approx0.1406\).

(d) The first three tosses are all tails: \(2^7/2^{10}=1/8\).

9 points

A box of 20 chocolates contains 8 dark, 7 milk and 5 white chocolates. You take 5 at random.

  1. Find the probability that exactly 2 are dark.
  2. Find the probability that at least one is white.
  3. Find the probability that all three kinds are among the 5 you take.
  4. You eat your 5 chocolates one at a time in random order. Before looking at any of them, find the probability that the first two you eat are both dark.
Solution

All \(\binom{20}5=15{,}504\) selections are equally likely.

(a) \(\dfrac{\binom82\binom{12}3}{\binom{20}5}=\dfrac{28\cdot220}{15{,}504}=\dfrac{6160}{15{,}504}\approx0.3973\).

(b) \(1-\dfrac{\binom{15}5}{\binom{20}5}=1-\dfrac{3003}{15{,}504}\approx0.8063\).

(c) Let \(D,M,W\) be the events that no dark, no milk, no white is chosen. \(P(D\cup M\cup W)\) by inclusion–exclusion counts \(\binom{12}5+\binom{13}5+\binom{15}5-\binom55-\binom75-\binom85=792+1287+3003-1-21-56=5004\) selections (the triple intersection is empty). So the answer is \(1-5004/15{,}504=10{,}500/15{,}504\approx0.6772\).

(d) The first two eaten are a random ordered pair from the 20 chocolates: \(\dfrac{8}{20}\cdot\dfrac{7}{19}=\dfrac{14}{95}\approx0.1474\).

9 points

A secretary types five letters to five different people and addresses five envelopes, then puts the letters into the envelopes completely at random, one letter per envelope.

  1. Find the probability that every letter is in its correct envelope.
  2. Find the probability that at least one letter is in its correct envelope.
  3. Find the probability that exactly 3 letters are in their correct envelopes.
  4. Find the probability that exactly one letter is in its correct envelope.
Solution

All \(5!=120\) assignments are equally likely. Let \(E_i\) be the event that letter \(i\) is correct; any \(r\) specified letters are all correct with probability \((5-r)!/5!\).

(a) \(1/120\).

(b) Inclusion–exclusion: \(\sum_{r=1}^5(-1)^{r+1}\binom5r\frac{(5-r)!}{5!}=1-\frac12+\frac16-\frac1{24}+\frac1{120}=\frac{76}{120}=\frac{19}{30}\approx0.6333\).

(c) Choose the 3 correct letters, \(\binom53=10\); the other 2 must be swapped (1 way): \(10/120=1/12\).

(d) Choose the correct letter (5 ways); the other 4 must all be wrong. By (b) applied to 4 letters, the number of all-wrong arrangements of 4 is \(4!\big(1-1+\frac12-\frac16+\frac1{24}\big)=9\). So \(5\cdot9/120=3/8\).

8 points

Six friends each pick a day of the week to go to the gym, independently and uniformly at random.

  1. Find the probability that all six pick different days.
  2. Find the probability that nobody picks Saturday or Sunday.
  3. Find the probability that at least one person picks a weekend day.
  4. Find the probability that exactly two different days of the week are picked.
Solution

There are \(7^6=117{,}649\) equally likely outcomes.

(a) \(\dfrac{7\cdot6\cdot5\cdot4\cdot3\cdot2}{7^6}=\dfrac{5040}{117{,}649}\approx0.0428\).

(b) \((5/7)^6=15{,}625/117{,}649\approx0.1328\).

(c) Complement of (b): \(1-0.1328=0.8672\).

(d) Choose the two days, \(\binom72=21\); each person picks one of them, but not all the same one: \(2^6-2=62\). So \(21\cdot62/7^6=1302/117{,}649\approx0.0111\).

8 points

An integer is chosen at random from 1 to 600.

  1. Find the probability that it is a perfect square or a perfect cube.
  2. Find the probability that it is divisible by 4 or by 6.
  3. Find the probability that it is divisible by exactly one of 4 and 6.
  4. Find the probability that it is an even perfect square.
Solution

(a) Squares: \(1^2,\dots,24^2\) (\(24^2=576\)), so 24. Cubes: \(1^3,\dots,8^3\) (\(8^3=512\)), so 8. Both means a sixth power: \(1, 64\), so 2. Count \(24+8-2=30\); probability \(30/600=1/20\).

(b) Multiples of 4: 150. Of 6: 100. Of both, i.e. of 12: 50. \((150+100-50)/600=200/600=1/3\).

(c) \(P(A\cup B)-P(AB)=(200-50)/600=1/4\).

(d) \((2k)^2\le600\iff k\le12\): 12 numbers, probability \(12/600=1/50\).

8 points

Ten different books, among them the three volumes of a trilogy, are put on a shelf in random order.

  1. Find the probability that the three volumes are next to each other.
  2. Find the probability that the volumes appear in the order 1, 2, 3 from left to right (not necessarily next to each other).
  3. Find the probability that the volumes are next to each other and in the order 1, 2, 3.
  4. Find the probability that no two of the three volumes are next to each other.
Solution

(a) Treat the trilogy as a block: \(\dfrac{8!\,3!}{10!}=\dfrac{6}{90}=\dfrac1{15}\).

(b) All \(3!\) relative orders are equally likely: \(1/6\).

(c) \(\dfrac{8!}{10!}=\dfrac1{90}\). (Equivalently \(\frac1{15}\cdot\frac16\).)

(d) The set of 3 positions occupied by the trilogy is equally likely to be any of \(\binom{10}3=120\) sets. Sets with no two adjacent: place 7 other books, choose 3 of the 8 gaps, \(\binom83=56\). Probability \(56/120=7/15\).

9 points

A 5-card hand is dealt from a well-shuffled standard deck.

  1. Find the probability that the hand contains at least one ace.
  2. Find the probability that it contains at least one ace or at least one king.
  3. Find the probability that it contains at least one ace and at least one king.
  4. Find the probability that all four suits appear in the hand.
Solution

There are \(\binom{52}5=2{,}598{,}960\) equally likely hands. Let \(A\) = at least one ace, \(K\) = at least one king.

(a) \(P(A)=1-\dfrac{\binom{48}5}{\binom{52}5}=1-\dfrac{1{,}712{,}304}{2{,}598{,}960}\approx0.3412\).

(b) \(P(A\cup K)=1-P(\text{no aces and no kings})=1-\dfrac{\binom{44}5}{\binom{52}5}=1-\dfrac{1{,}086{,}008}{2{,}598{,}960}\approx0.5821\).

(c) \(P(AK)=P(A)+P(K)-P(A\cup K)=2(0.34116)-0.58214\approx0.1002\).

(d) One suit appears twice and the others once: choose that suit (4), its 2 cards \(\binom{13}2\), and one card of each other suit \(13^3\): \(\dfrac{4\cdot78\cdot2197}{2{,}598{,}960}=\dfrac{685{,}464}{2{,}598{,}960}\approx0.2637\).
